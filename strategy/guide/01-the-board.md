# The board

Twenty-three stopping points joined by forty-two pathways. Every line is the
same length, so each point sits exactly one step from each of its neighbours,
and a move is always a step to an adjacent point.

![The board, with every point named.](fig:7889280,1573248,0,0|labels)

The points fall into four groups, and the names in this guide follow them.

**A1** is the hub. It touches all six points of the inner hexagon and nothing
else. Six connections is the highest count on the board, and it costs more than
it pays. The chapter on board control comes back to it.

**B1 to B6** form the inner hexagon. Each one touches the hub, two neighbours
around the hexagon, and two points on the outer ring: five connections each.

**C1 to C12** form the outer ring, a dodecagon. Connections here vary between
three and four.

**D1 to D4** are the four home points, two at each end of the board. They are
the quietest squares on it, with two connections apiece.

## The two home bases

White owns C1, C2, D1 and D2. Black owns C7, C8, D3 and D4. Each base is a
square of four points closed by four pathways.

A home base does three separate jobs, and it is worth keeping all three in mind
because they pull in different directions:

1. A cob standing on its own base may move in any direction, not only forward.
   Your own base is the one place a cob can retreat from.
2. A cob that lands on the opponent's base is promoted to a rok at once.
3. A cob captured while standing on its own base stays a cob. It changes owner
   and keeps its rank.

## Writing a move

A move is written as the point it leaves and the point it reaches, joined by a
hyphen. `C1-B1` moves whatever stands on C1 to B1. Captures are not marked,
because a capture is a consequence of the move rather than a choice.

One move looks odd at first: `D3-D3`, a point moving to itself. That is the
forced promotion of a dead cob, and the endgame chapter explains when it
happens.

## The board is symmetric twice over

Turn the board through half a circle and White's half lands exactly on Black's.
Under that rotation A1 maps to itself, each B point maps to the one opposite,
each C point to the one six around, and the two home bases swap. Tarati is
therefore perfectly balanced between the colours, and the only real difference
between the two players is that White moves first.

That single fact is worth holding onto. Any time one colour appears to be
winning far more often than the other, something other than the game is
producing it.

There is a second symmetry, and it is the more useful one at the board. Fold
the board along the line running through B1, A1 and B4. The left half lands on
the right half, and the colours stay where they are. The opening position is
unchanged by this fold.

![The fold axis runs through B1, A1 and B4. The two halves are mirror images.](fig:7889280,1573248,0,0|mark=B1,A1,B4)

So the four moves White can legally make on move one are two moves seen
twice:

| Move | Mirror image |
|------|--------------|
| C1-C12 | C2-C3 |
| C1-B1 | C2-B1 |

`C1-C12` steps along the outer ring. `C1-B1` steps inward toward the hub. Every
opening in Tarati begins with one of those two ideas, and each can be played
from either side of the board. Half the opening theory you might expect
does not exist.
