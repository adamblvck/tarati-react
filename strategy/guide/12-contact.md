# There is no quiet approach

Here is a rule you will not find in the rulebook, because it is not a rule. It is
a consequence, and it is the single most surprising thing about how Tarati
actually plays.

**You cannot walk up to an enemy piece. If you arrive beside one, you take it.**

Read the striking rule again and it is obvious in hindsight. A move strikes every
enemy adjacent to your destination that was not already adjacent to your origin.
"An enemy adjacent to where I am going that was not adjacent to where I was" is
just a long way of saying "an enemy I have newly come into contact with". The
condition for striking and the condition for approaching are the same condition.

We checked it exhaustively. Of 52,267 legal moves examined across the corpus,
10,450 made new contact with an enemy piece. The number of those that struck
nothing: **zero**. And running it the other way, across 39,107 actual strikes in
recorded games, the number delivered by a piece that was already touching its
victim: **zero**.

## What follows: the standoff

If contact can only be created by a strike, then contact that already exists is
permanent, and it is *safe*.

Your piece and an enemy piece are standing next to each other. Can it take you?
No. It is already beside you, so moving to any square still beside you strikes
nothing; the rule requires new contact and there is none to be had. Can you take
it? Also no, for exactly the same reason.

Two pieces in contact are frozen with respect to each other. Neither can ever
harm the other from where they stand. They can shuffle around each other all day.

![White's cob on B4 is touching two black cobs at once, on B5 and C8. It cannot take either of them and neither can take it. In this whole position, four pieces apiece, there is not a single move for either side that strikes anything.](fig:5914928,1573136,0,1|mark=B4,B5,C8)

This has a defensive use that most players never find. If a piece of yours is
being hunted, one way out is not to run. It is to be *taken into contact* by the
piece hunting it, or to accept a position where you are already touching. An
attacker in contact with you has spent itself. It must leave, go somewhere it is
no longer adjacent, and come back by a different road before it can hurt you, and
that takes at least two moves you can see coming.

## What follows: attacks are built from a distance

The other half is what the board-control chapter called setting up a strike, and
now we can say exactly why it works that way.

To take a piece, you must arrive from somewhere you were not. So the piece that
takes it is never the piece already next to it. It is a piece **two steps away**,
or a piece that steps back and then in. Every attack in Tarati is launched from
just outside contact.

That gives you a clean way to read a position for danger. Forget your opponent's
pieces that are touching yours, which are inert against you. Look at the ones
sitting one square off, with a path to an empty point beside your piece. Those
are the live threats, and they are the ones a club player consistently fails to
see, because they do not look like they are attacking anything.

**The two-step scan.** For each of your pieces that matters, look at its doors.
For each door, ask which enemy pieces can reach that square in one move, and
whether any of them are not already touching your piece. If the answer is yes,
the piece is takeable next move. If the answer is no, it is safe this turn no
matter how threatening the position looks.

## What follows: contact is the residue of a fight

There is one more consequence, and it is useful for reading a board you have just
sat down at.

Since no one can walk into contact, every adjacency between opposing pieces on
the board arrived there one of three ways: a strike happened there, a piece slid
from one square beside the enemy to another, or a nearby piece changed colour and
turned a friend into an enemy. Contact is always the residue of something that
already happened.

So a cluster of touching pieces is not a battle about to start. It is a battle
that already finished, and the pieces in it are, for the moment, spent. The next
strike will come from somewhere else, from the quiet part of the board where a
piece is sitting two steps from a door nobody is watching.
