# Striking

You capture by arriving. Move a piece so that it lands next to an enemy, and
that enemy is struck: it stays on its point but flips to your colour and joins
your side. Nothing is removed from the board. A game of Tarati is won by
converting pieces, not by clearing them.

One move can convert several pieces at once. Every enemy standing next to your
piece's destination is struck together.

![Black plays C8-C7 and strikes on both sides at once, flipping the white pieces on C6 and D3.](fig:3691032,2101272,2621952,1|arrow=C8-C7|mark=C6,D3)

## The rule that makes Tarati Tarati

There is one condition on striking, and it is the whole character of the game:

> You cannot strike an enemy you were already standing next to. To capture a
> piece, you must arrive from a point that was not adjacent to it.

Read that once and it sounds like a footnote. Watch it decide a single move and
it stops being one.

![White's B1 sits next to two black pieces at once: A1 and B3.](fig:2637979,524418,0,0|mark=A1,B3)

White's cob on B1 is touching two black pieces, A1 and B3. White plays B1-B2.
Now B2 is next to both of them, so both look captured.

Only B3 flips.

![After B1-B2: B3 has flipped to white, A1 is still black. Same move, opposite outcomes.](fig:2637981,524428,0,1|mark=A1,B3)

A1 was already B1's neighbour before the move, so the rule spares it: it stays
black. B3 was also B1's neighbour, and it flips anyway, because B2 reaches it
along a line that B1 did not command. Both pieces began adjacent to the mover.
One is taken and one is not, and the only difference is the geometry of the
arrival.

This is why a strike is a thing you set up rather than a thing you spot. Sitting
next to an enemy accomplishes nothing. You have to step away and come back by
another road, or bring a different piece from distance.

## What it costs the attacker

The pre-adjacency rule protects the defender, so it also disciplines the
attacker. Crowding your pieces against the enemy line feels aggressive and
achieves very little: every piece already in contact is a piece that cannot
capture what it touches. The threats on the board are the arrivals you have not
made yet, from points the enemy is not watching.

The chapter on tactics puts numbers to this. For now, hold the shape of it: in
Tarati, standing next to something is the opposite of threatening it.

## Promotion

A cob moves only forward, toward the enemy. Push one all the way onto the
opponent's home base and it is promoted on arrival to a rok, which moves in any
direction for the rest of the game.

![A black cob reaches D1, deep in white's home base, and becomes a rok.](fig:1073307,1048601,0,1|arrow=C1-D1|mark=D1)

Promotion changes what a piece is for. A cob is a one-way runner that must keep
advancing or die where it stands. A rok is free. It can retreat to defend, swing
across the board to strike from a new angle, and set up the arrivals that a cob
can never come back to make. Most decisive attacks in the corpus are led by a
rok, for exactly that reason.

There is one twist, and it protects the defender again. A cob struck while
standing on its own home base does not promote. It changes colour and
stays a cob, stranded on the wrong side of the board, only able to move further
from where it now needs to go. The endgame chapter returns to these stranded
pieces, because a game is often decided by them.
