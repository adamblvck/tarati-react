# The endgame

You do not win Tarati by clearing the board. You win by owning it. Every enemy
piece you strike changes colour and joins you, so the natural end of a game is
not an empty board but a board that has turned entirely to your side. Knowing
how that conversion arrives, and how it can stall, is what wins close games.

## How games end

Across more than a million recorded games, the endings break down like this:

| How the game ended | Share |
|--------------------|:-----:|
| Total conversion (every piece one colour) | 82.9% |
| Opponent has no legal move | 10.6% |
| Threefold repetition (draw) | 6.2% |
| Move limit or fifty-move draw | 0.3% |

More than four games in five end by total conversion. The board does not empty;
it flips. This single fact shapes the whole endgame: your target is not the
enemy's last piece but the enemy's last **point**, and every capture brings that
end one piece closer while making you stronger and them weaker at the same time.

## Conversion cascades

Because a capture flips a piece rather than removing it, and because one move can
strike every enemy standing next to its destination, the end often arrives all
at once. A single well-placed arrival can turn three pieces over and finish the
game on the spot.

![White plays B2-A1 and strikes B4, B5 and B6 together. Every black piece flips at once and the game is over.](seq:155772,155660,8296,0|arrow=B2-A1|mark=B4,B5,B6|sub=White to play;;155769,155769,8296,1|sub=all white: game over)

Here a white cob steps to the hub and lands beside three black pieces at once.
None of them was adjacent to the cob before it moved, so all three are struck,
all three flip, and the board is entirely white. The game ends in a single move.

This is the centre used correctly, and it is worth setting beside the warning in
the board-control chapter. Sitting on the hub is a liability because it can be
struck from six directions. Passing through the hub to strike from it is
devastating for exactly the same reason: from the centre you stand next to
everything. The rule that makes the hub dangerous to occupy makes it lethal to
attack from. The centre is a place you arrive, strike, and win, not a place you
wait.

## The rok wins endgames

The piece that delivers these conversions is almost always a rok. A cob can only
push forward, so once it has advanced it can never turn to strike a piece behind
or beside it, and late in the game the pieces you need to convert are scattered
in every direction. A rok goes anywhere. It can cross the board to reach a lone
holdout, retreat to flip a piece that slipped past, and, above all, leave a
square and return to strike a target it was already next to. Promote a cob to a
rok and you have turned a one-way runner into the tool that finishes games. If
you are ahead as the board empties, get a rok and hunt with it.

## Jamming: the game's quiet decider

Not every advantage converts. The forward-only cob has a fatal weakness, and in
close endgames it decides more games than any tactic.

![A black cob is stranded on D2, at the far edge of White's home. It has no square ahead of it and can never move again.](fig:7872786,6299904,532480,0|mark=D2)

A cob that reaches the far edge with no point ahead of it is **dead**. It cannot
advance, because there is nothing to advance to, and it cannot retreat, because
cobs never do. It sits on the board as your piece in name only, contributing
nothing, blocking nothing useful, waiting to be counted against you.

Measured across the labelled positions, a dead cob is worth about a third of a
piece **against** its owner even while it still counts as material. You can be
ahead on the piece count and behind in reality because one of your pieces has
walked into a corner it can never leave. The most common way this happens is
cruel: a cob captured while sitting on its own home base keeps its rank instead
of promoting, and is reborn as an enemy cob at the one edge of the board where a
cob is worthless.

The practical lessons are two. First, watch where your cobs are heading. A cob
driven to the far edge without a promotion square waiting is a cob you are about
to lose the use of. Second, when you are defending, you can aim for this: force
your opponent's cobs forward into dead ends, and a material deficit can turn into
a win as their pieces strand themselves one by one.

## Playing the endgame

Draw the threads together. You are trying to convert the whole board, so keep
your captures coming and count points, not pieces. A rok is your conversion
engine, so make one and use its freedom. The hub and the inner ring, dangerous
to occupy, are where your decisive arrivals come from, so strike through the
centre rather than sitting in it. And keep one eye on the forward-only cobs, both
yours and your opponent's, because the game is often decided not by a brilliant
capture but by whose pieces quietly ran out of road.
